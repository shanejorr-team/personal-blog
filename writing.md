Before she boarded the plane back to Colombia, our 16-year-old daughter-to-be handed my wife her Quinceañera dress—one of her most treasured possessions. "Please keep it safe for me," she said.

That was December 2024. We haven't been allowed to see or speak with her since.

My wife Jessica and I spent four weeks that month hosting two Colombian siblings, a 14-year-old boy and a 16-year-old girl, through a program that connects children in foster care with potential adoptive families. Within days, they felt like they had always been part of our family. When they left, our house felt empty. We started the formal adoption process the very next day.

We are, along with hundreds of other American families, now caught in legal limbo.

On January 14, 2026, the State Department issued a memo titled "Immigrant Visa Processing Updates for Nationalities at High Risk of Public Benefits Usage." The memo pauses all immigrant visas for nationals of 75 countries. Because the pause applies to all immigrant visas, it sweeps in IH-3 and IR-3 adoption visas, which are two of the visas American families use to bring their internationally adopted children home.

The stated justification is to ensure immigrants from these countries "do not utilize welfare in the United States or become a public charge." The phrase "public charge" comes from the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) and bars entry to immigrants who might become primarily dependent on government assistance.

This is a valid legal and policy justification for restricting immigration. But it makes no sense for international adoptions because it is legally impossible for these children to become public charges.

Under the Child Citizenship Act, children entering the United States on IH-3 or IR-3 adoption visas become American citizens the moment they step on U.S. soil. Not weeks later. Not after paperwork. Immediately.

The "public charge" statute in the INA, however, only applies to non-citizens. The statute's plain language makes this clear: "Any alien who … is likely at any time to become a public charge is inadmissible." The INA defines "alien" as "any person not a citizen or national of the United States."

The conclusion follows directly. Children who enter on IH-3 and IR-3 visas become United States citizens upon arrival. Citizens are not aliens. The public charge statute applies only to aliens. Therefore, it is legally impossible for these children to become public charges under the statute.

This isn't a novel legal theory. The State Department's own Foreign Affairs Manual instructs consular officers that "the public charge concern will no longer apply to the applicant once the immigrant acquires citizenship" and to consider "acquisition of citizenship immediately upon admission." The government's own guidance already recognizes that adoption visas are different.

The government's own vetting process provides an additional safeguard. Every adoptive parent must prove financial capacity before being approved to adopt. USCIS reviews income, assets, and expenses before issuing the I-800A approval that allows families to proceed. By the time a family reaches the visa stage, the government has already certified they can financially support their children. The concern animating this pause has already been addressed.

The current policy creates real harm for no legal benefit. According to State Department statistics, American families completed 513 adoptions from affected countries in fiscal year 2024. This amounts to nearly half of all intercountry adoptions that year. Colombia alone accounted for 200 of those children.

Now, families who have completed years of paperwork, passed FBI background checks, opened their finances to government scrutiny, and been matched with children are being told to wait indefinitely. Children who have already bonded with their American families remain in foster care abroad. The memo offers no timeline, no exception process, and no explanation of how a public charge rationale applies to future citizens.

We are not asking for special treatment. We are asking the State Department to recognize what the law already says: children who become American citizens upon arrival cannot be public charges. This appears to be an oversight, not a deliberate policy to separate American families from their children. A simple clarification exempting IH-3 and IR-3 adoption visas would align the memo with existing law and reunite families who have done everything right.

Hundreds of American families are waiting. They have followed every rule. Their children are waiting too, some not yet knowing that a family has chosen them. The Quinceañera dress in our closet belongs to one of those children. We are asking the State Department to let us keep our promise to bring her home.
