In a world that is culturally globalizing, it is rare to find distinct national holidays. Holidays
whose traditions and customs have not seeped through porous cultural borders into other nations.
Dia de las Velitas ("Day of the Little Candles"), however, is a distincly Colombian national holiday.

### What is Dia de las Velitas?

Colombians celebrate the holiday December 7th, serving as an informal start to the Christmas season.
As the name implies, the holiday centers around candles. People place lit candles and faroles
(lanterns) in public places like parks and along streets, and also on their patios.

<InlineFigure src={photo5} alt="Gathering around a candle on Dia de las Velitas" caption="Gathering around a candle on Dia de las Velitas" />

Historically, Dia de las Velitas marked the eve of the feast of the immaculate conception. The feast,
which is celebrated throughout Latin America on December 8th, honors the Catholic orthodoxy that the
Virgin Mary was conceived without original sin. Within Colombia's religious tradition, devotees lit
candles in honor of Mary the day before the feast. This tradition became Dia de las Velitas.[^1]

History, however, tells you little about a holiday's modern cultural significance. Think Halloween,
Labor Day, the Fourth of July, and almost any other US holiday. Similairly, the only link Dia de las
Velitas maintains with the past is candles.

<InlineFigure src={velitas4} alt="Family gathering around candles" caption="Candles lit for Dia de las Velitas in Jardin, Colombia" />

But, even the meaning of the canldes have changed. Few Colombian's light candles in honor of the
Mary's lack of original sin. Now, the candles might represent hope, faith, or the coming of the
Christmas season.

Or, they simply act as the social glue to bring people together. In this regard, the candles serve a
similar role as fireworks for the Fourth of July. Something fun to do in community.

### Dia de las velitas in Jardin

By pure serendipidy, I spent Dia de las Velitas in Jardin, Colombia - a small town of 15,000 people.
The town is a winding three hour bus ride from Medellin, with the route taking you through the heart
of the Antioquian Andes.

Jardin is an officially designated pueblo patrimonio and shares characteristics with many such
towns:[^2] colorful buildings lining cobblestone streets, botique restaurants and coffee shops on
every corner, and an old but well maintained church anchoring the town's central plaza.

<InlineFigure src={photo13} alt="Colorful buildings lining the road that leads to the Basilica Menor de la Inmaculada Concepcion" caption="Colorful buildings lining the road that leads to the Basilica Menor de la Inmaculada Concepcion" />

Being close to Medellin, many paisas visit Jardin on weekends and holidays. Due to this, the town
was packed with travelors for Dia de las Velitas.

I stepped off the bus and into the central plaza at nightfall and was instantly immersed in candles,
faroles, and people. Every walkway in the plaza was lined with candles. Families occupied every
bench, glowing in the ambient light of faroles.

<InlineFigure src={velitas6} alt="Jardin's central plaza crowded with people while the Basilica Menor de la Inmaculada Concepcion shines in the background" caption="Jardin's central plaza crowded with people while the Basilica Menor de la Inmaculada Concepcion shines in the background" />

The steps in front of the basilica were especially beautiful. Every step, every inch, was covered
with brightly lit candles and faroles. They provided a beautiful foreground to the lit up church.

<InlineFigure src={velitas2} alt="Faroles covering the steps of the Basilica Menor de la Inmaculada Concepcion" caption="Candles and faroles covering the steps of the Basilica Menor de la Inmaculada Concepcion." />

The whole town seemed to gather in the central plaza. Families and friends came together, lit
candles, and then sat around the candles to chat. Couples held hands, parents ate street food such
as salsipapas, and children acted as children.

<InlineFigure src={velitas1} alt="Families gathered around candles in Jardin's central plaza on Dia de las Velitas" caption="Families gathered around candles in Jardin's central plaza on Dia de las Velitas." />

Gleelful children used lighting candles as an excuse to play with fire. They lit faroles, paper, and
anything else that might burn; conducting science experiments and taunting / scaring their friends
with burning objects. In a universal sign of flirting, one boy lit a piece of paper and chased a
girl with it. I'm still curious whether his tactics secured him his first kiss.

Residents celebrated the holiday beyond the town plaza. Businesses on side-streets set candles in
front of their shops, as shopkeepers and patrons gathered around the candles gossiping. Not to be
outdone, homeowners lined their balconies with candles and faroles.

<InlineFigure src={photo4} alt="Faroles lining the street" caption="Faroles lining the street" />

On the surface, Dia de las Velitas was about candles. In reality, however, it was about community.
Candles were secondary actors.

People didn't just light candles and silently stare at them, hypnotized by their stochastic flames.
They talked, laughed, and played. They came together in public spaces with no agenda beyond coming
together in public spaces.

<InlineFigure src={photo6} alt="Community gathered together in the glow of candles and faroles during Dia de las Velitas" caption="Community gathered together in the glow of candles and faroles during Dia de las Velitas." />

This concept - coming together in a communal space to do nothing but chat - is a rarity in the
United States. In Colombia, however, it is a common past time, usually accompanied by a Poker or
Club Colombia beer. It is one of the many dimensions of Colombian culture that the United States
should embrace.

[^1]: The full history is more nuanced. Pope Pius IX issued a papal bull defining the immaculate
conception - the belief Mary was conceived without original sin - as orthodoxy on December 8, 1854.
The day prior, Colombians lit candles in anticipation of the announcement. And with that, Dia de las
Velitas started.
[^2]: Pueblos Patrimonios are a group of 17 Colombian towns officially designated so by the Colombian
government for their exceptional cultural, architectural, and historical heritage.
